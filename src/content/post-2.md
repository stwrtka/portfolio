**SURPRISES** <br>
Throughout the research for this map, I found a lot of surprises that popped up. For instance, I found out that Kid Pix was actually the first bit-map software released that was targeted to children. Additionally, Craig Hickman’s life after Kid Pix. I already knew he had an artistic background and assumed that he would have moved more into programming related fields. However, I took a second to think about why he wouldn’t have taken that step and came to the realization that Computer Science is more theory-based.Besides Kid Pix, another surprise I found came from the drawing applications before Kid Pix. All the user interfaces had similar layouts that squished aspects together. However, there was a trend to place the toolbox (brushes, erasers, paintbrushes, etc.) on the left side. Additionally, the user interfaces stopped providing patterns as an option, like MacPaint and shifted towards limited colours, a colour wheel/box or both.  <br><br>

**MAP MAKING PROCESS** <br>
Creating this map taught me a lot about the map-making process and the benefits of using a map as a storytelling tool. One thing that stood out to me is that maps allow the creator more freedom in how they tell a story compared to a timeline. Depending on the software, creators have a choice in how they present the information: a static map with a guided path or a map that allows the reader to create a story based on what the creator has mapped out. These options differ significantly from timelines, as they are presented in chronological order. However, the increased freedom can result in a multitude of additional concerns, such as determining the appropriate number of points to include and how the information on each point should be presented. If there are too many points, the map can become cluttered and difficult to read; if there are too few points, it becomes even harder to understand the story. <br><br>

**REFLECTION: TIMELINE VS MAP** <br>
In my previous blog, I utilized a timeline that focused on the chronological history of Kid Pix. From how Craig Hickman even got the idea, Kid Pix’s first company, to how Kid Pix is today. After some reflection, I pinpointed two main issues with how I presented my topic in the timeline. One, the timeline hyper-focused on the corporations of Kid Pix rather than the actual application. In my opinion, framing the story in that way never showed readers why Kid Pix was so great. Two, focusing only on Kid Pix would become a problem later on due to the limited information on it. Keeping these two things in mind, I wanted to tell a different story for my map that addressed these issues. Looking back at the applications before Kid Pix, there’s a noticeable pattern in how the application looks, and early Kid Pix was no exception to this pattern. However, this UI design choice was not child-friendly; there were either too many options or too few options (Delta Drawing). Many of these applications claimed to be easy to use, but the difficulty varied from person to person. Kid Pix started with a similar notion but focused on the goal of creating something that didn’t need a guide, something intuitive.  From my experience working in graphic design and utilizing programs such as Photoshop and Illustrator, Kid Pix really reinforces the idea to me that intuition-based design will always be timeless. Often, when trying new programs, I had to check online how to do relatively simple things, such as finding out what command the lasso tool is or how to clip layers together. After using both storytelling tools to explore different perspectives of the same topic, I prefer maps more. Maps felt a lot less restrictive, despite being harder to gather information for, it forces you to think big and avoid really small details.
